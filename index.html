In 2017, I was a school student in Nagercoil with little more than a vague sense that nature was worth preserving. My parents enrolled me in the Kumari Arivial Peravai (KAP) Young Scientists Programme — a rigorous, year-long environmental science initiative affiliated with CSIR and the MHRD, Government of India. I did not choose it. I was not yet someone who would have chosen it.

What the programme gave me was not simply knowledge — it gave me proximity to consequence. Standing at the edge of a coastal estuary, counting mangrove saplings in a deteriorating habitat, I encountered for the first time the gap between environmental data and political will. The ecosystems I was documenting were degrading not because no one cared, but because no one was measuring — and because those who were measuring had no pathway to those who could act.

Selected from over 500 students and evaluated by a six-member panel of scientists, I was ultimately honoured with the Young Scientist Award at the Gandhigram Rural Institute Deemed University Symposium (MHRD-funded, Government of India) in May 2018. But the award was secondary to what the programme had done to my understanding of the world.

I authored the chapter "Channels and Rivers in Kanyakumari District" in the programme's published book, Rescue the Planet Earth — documenting local freshwater ecosystems and waterway geography. This chapter directly informed a subsequent community-led channel restoration project in Kanyakumari. For the first time, I had seen how evidence, carefully gathered and clearly communicated, could translate into community action.

The following year, I returned — not as a participant, but as a Students' Guide and Team Supporter for the next cohort under the theme "Land and Life." I mentored junior participants through tree plantation, water body studies, and innovative project design. I was eighteen. I was already teaching what I had learned.

That cycle — field observation to evidence to governance to action to mentorship — became the intellectual and moral template for everything I have built since.

03 Research Philosophy

Research as an instrument
of governance and equity.

My approach to research is shaped by a conviction formed in 2017 and refined over nearly a decade: environmental science that does not inform governance is incomplete. The field surveys I conducted as a sixteen-year-old documented degrading ecosystems in precise detail. The ecosystems continued to degrade. The problem was never insufficient data — it was insufficient translation from evidence to action.

This conviction has guided every research decision I have made since. My published paper on AI-based cognitive and emotional monitoring is not a computer science paper that happens to mention social welfare — it is a public health paper that uses machine learning as its instrument. The question it asks is fundamentally a governance question: how can underserved communities access mental health support at scale?

Similarly, my patent for HELIO-CASE did not begin with a technology solution looking for a problem. It began with the observation that environmental monitoring equipment — the kind used in field research — is power-dependent in ways that exclude the most climate-vulnerable communities from the data generation process. The invention is a response to an equity gap.

My sustainability platforms — Eco Score and ESG Ranking — reflect the same principle. Carbon footprint calculators exist in abundance. India-specific carbon calculators, calibrated to India's National Determined Contributions and aligned with behavioural science literature on climate communication, do not. I built what was missing, not what was popular.

If I were to articulate a research philosophy in a single sentence: I am interested in the space between what we measure and what we govern — and specifically in building the tools, frameworks, and institutional capacities that close that gap.

01

Evidence → Governance Translation

Research is most valuable when it narrows the distance between scientific evidence and policy action. Every project I undertake asks: who needs this data, and how will they use it?

02

Equity-Centred Design

Sustainability tools that work only in high-resource environments perpetuate the inequities that cause environmental degradation. I design for the communities most affected, not most affluent.

03

Interdisciplinary Rigour

Climate governance requires fluency in ecology, data science, policy analysis, and behavioural science simultaneously. I am deliberately building competence across all four.

04

Local Knowledge, Global Frameworks

The data gap in South Asian sustainability governance is not just technical — it is contextual. Global frameworks must be translated into local realities. I am positioned to do both.

Application No. 202541040849 · Filed: 28 April 2025 · Published: 30 May 2025 · IP India (U/S 11A)

HELIO-CASE addresses a practical barrier in environmental monitoring and sustainability data collection: the power dependency of field equipment in climate-vulnerable, off-grid communities. The device integrates sun-powered energy harvesting with thermal management and heat-resistant protection — enabling durable, climate-resilient deployment of monitoring hardware in remote and resource-constrained environments where conventional power infrastructure is absent or unreliable.

Filed on 28 April 2025 and officially published under Section 11A of the Patents Act by IP India on 30 May 2025 — the patent has cleared the publication stage and is now publicly verifiable on the IP India portal (Application No. 202541040849). Publication under S.11A means the invention has been formally disclosed to the public and is no longer merely "applied for" — it is on record with the Government of India as a substantive intellectual property contribution.

The motivation is explicitly environmental governance-oriented. The communities most affected by climate change are frequently those with the least capacity to generate the environmental data that informs the policies designed to protect them. HELIO-CASE is a partial response to this structural asymmetry — enabling data collection where it is most needed, without dependence on grid electricity that many climate-vulnerable regions lack.

The governance gap this addresses: India has committed to ambitious Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement — a 2-ton-per-person carbon budget by 2030. Yet no publicly accessible, India-calibrated tool existed to translate this national commitment into individual accountability. Generic Western calculators use US or European emission factors, misrepresenting India's electricity grid (predominantly coal), dietary norms, and transport infrastructure, producing misleading results for Indian users.

EcoScore fills this gap through a four-step assessment covering transport, electricity consumption, diet, and aviation. It uses 2023 IPCC emission factors applied to India-specific grid data, compares the user's footprint against both the Indian average (1.9 tons/year) and global average (4.0 tons/year), and identifies which behaviour is contributing most to the user's total emissions.

The recommendations engine moves beyond generic advice. Rather than "plant trees," it produces specific, quantified suggestions based on the user's individual data — for example: "switching to bus transport three days per week saves 0.8 tons annually." This behavioural specificity reflects the climate communication literature, which consistently shows that personalised, quantified recommendations outperform generic messaging in driving behaviour change.

A gamified badge system — including "Paris Achiever" for users below the 2-ton target — applies positive reinforcement design principles to climate literacy. The Paris Agreement target of 2 tons per person by 2030 is made concrete, measurable, and personally relevant for every user who completes the assessment.

What this demonstrates for Erasmus Committees

This is not a student who writes essays about caring for the environment. This is a student who identified a specific data gap in India's climate accountability infrastructure, understood its policy context (NDC commitments, Paris targets), and built a publicly deployed tool to address it. The combination of policy literacy, technical execution, and behavioural science application is precisely the interdisciplinary competence that MESPOM and CCD-STeDe programmes seek to develop.

The governance gap this addresses: ESG reporting is increasingly central to global sustainability governance — yet South Asian implementation remains opaque. Most ESG ratings are locked behind commercial paywalls (MSCI, Sustainalytics). SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Reporting (BRSR) mandate is relatively recent, and compliance data is scattered across company annual reports that require significant expertise to interpret. Civil society actors, researchers, and smaller investors have no practical tool for comparative analysis.

The platform tracks 10 major Indian corporations across a weighted ESG composite — Environmental (33%), Social (33%), Governance (34%) — with letter grades from A+ to C. Four years of historical data (2022–2025) enables trend analysis, not just snapshot comparison. Users can search and filter by industry, compare two or three companies simultaneously with overlay trend charts and radar visualisations, and view how each company performs against its industry benchmark average — a feature that provides regulatory context that raw scores alone cannot.

A machine learning layer uses Linear Regression fitted to each company's four-year score history to project performance one year forward. Accuracy across tested companies is 85–95% R² — appropriate for a domain where change is gradual and structurally driven, and where sudden discontinuities (scandal, major policy shift) are explicitly noted as a limitation. The model also classifies each company as "improving" or "declining" with a confidence score — a governance signal for investors and civil society actors monitoring corporate accountability trajectories.

The data itself required manual compilation from company annual reports — a deliberate choice given commercial data paywalls. This process deepened my understanding of how Indian corporations self-report sustainability performance, where disclosure gaps are systematically concentrated (small-cap companies, Social category), and how BRSR compliance varies across sectors. This is the kind of hands-on policy data literacy that no coursework alone provides.

What this demonstrates for Erasmus Committees

This project sits at the precise intersection of ESG policy frameworks (GRI, TCFD, SEBI BRSR), data science (ML prediction, visualisation), and civil society accountability. It required understanding both the technical infrastructure of corporate sustainability reporting and the governance ecosystem in which that reporting operates. MUNDUS MAPP and MERGED committees evaluating candidates for policy-technical hybrid roles will recognise this combination as directly applicable.

A Note on Methodology

Both projects were built with deliberate technical constraints that reflect governance realities. EcoScore uses CSV-based data storage — not because I could not build a database, but because the tool needs to be maintainable and auditable by non-technical sustainability officers who may eventually inherit it. ESG Ranking uses serverless deployment because regulatory context in India may require rapid updates as SEBI's BRSR requirements evolve, and a serverless architecture reduces the operational burden of keeping the tool current. Technical decisions in sustainability tools are governance decisions. I made them with that understanding.

09 Why Erasmus Mundus

A deliberate choice,
not a prestigious default.

I have been asked by well-meaning advisors why I am applying to Erasmus Mundus rather than a single-university master's programme. The answer is structural, not aspirational.

The problems I want to work on do not respect national borders. Climate governance is inherently multilateral. ESG standards are globally contested. Environmental data systems are only as useful as the international frameworks that give them meaning. A master's programme that takes place in one country, through one institutional lens, is inadequate preparation for work that requires fluency in multiple regulatory traditions, policy cultures, and scientific communities simultaneously.

Erasmus Mundus programmes are not simply prestigious — they are architecturally suited to the kind of practitioner I need to become. The consortium model means that I will study climate policy in one European regulatory context, environmental governance in another, and sustainability transitions in a third. The peer cohort will include professionals from every continent directly affected by the challenges we are studying.

There is also a more personal reason. I grew up in Nagercoil, in the southernmost district of India — a place that is simultaneously one of the most biodiverse regions in the country and one of the most climate-vulnerable. The communities I want to serve are not in European capitals. They are in places like Kanyakumari, in the Maldives, in Bangladesh, in sub-Saharan Africa. The multilateral networks built through an Erasmus Mundus education are precisely those through which the interests of these communities are most effectively advanced.

I am applying for a fully funded scholarship because I cannot fund this education from within India at the level it requires — and because the funding model of Erasmus Mundus reflects a commitment to selecting candidates on merit and potential rather than financial privilege. I believe I meet that standard.

01

Interdisciplinary Architecture

The problems I work on — climate governance, ESG policy, environmental data — require simultaneous fluency in science, policy, economics, and law. No single discipline is sufficient.

02

Multilateral Networks

The institutions I aspire to contribute to — UN system, World Bank, UNDP — operate through exactly the multilateral relationships that Erasmus consortium structures cultivate.

03

Global South Representation

Erasmus cohorts typically lack candidates with direct experience of environmental governance challenges in South Asia. I bring a perspective — and a network — that these programmes need as much as I need them.

04

Merit-Based Access

The fully funded scholarship model ensures that the best candidates are selected regardless of economic background. This reflects the values of equity and access that drive my work in sustainability.
