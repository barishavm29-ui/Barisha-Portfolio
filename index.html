I did not choose it. My parents enrolled me in the Kumari Arivial Peravai (KAP) Young Scientists Programme in 2017 — a year-long environmental science initiative affiliated with CSIR and the MHRD, Government of India. What the programme gave me was proximity to consequence: standing at the edge of a coastal estuary, counting mangrove saplings in a deteriorating habitat, I encountered for the first time the gap between environmental data and political will. The ecosystems I was documenting were degrading not because no one cared, but because no one was measuring — and because those who were measuring had no pathway to those who could act.

Selected from over 500 students, I received the Young Scientist Award at the Gandhigram Rural Institute Deemed University Symposium (MHRD-funded, May 2018) and authored the chapter "Channels and Rivers in Kanyakumari District" in the programme's published book — documentation that directly informed a community-led channel restoration project. The following year, I returned as a Students' Guide for the next cohort, mentoring junior participants through the same field work. That cycle — field observation to evidence to action to mentorship — became the template for everything I have built since.

HELIO-CASE: Sun-Powered Charging with Heat-Resistant Protection
Application No. 202541040849 · Filed: 28 April 2025 · Published: 30 May 2025 · IP India (U/S 11A)

HELIO-CASE addresses a practical barrier in sustainability data collection: the power dependency of field equipment in climate-vulnerable, off-grid communities. The device integrates sun-powered energy harvesting with thermal management and heat-resistant protection, enabling durable deployment of monitoring hardware where grid electricity is absent. Filed 28 April 2025 and officially published under Section 11A of the Patents Act by IP India on 30 May 2025 (Application No. 202541040849 — publicly verifiable on ipindia.gov.in). The motivation is a governance one: the communities most exposed to climate change are frequently those least able to generate the environmental data that informs policies designed to protect them.

The governance gap: India has committed to a 2-ton-per-person carbon budget by 2030 under its NDCs, yet no publicly accessible, India-calibrated footprint tool existed aligned with these commitments. Generic Western calculators misrepresent India's predominantly coal-based electricity grid, dietary norms, and transport infrastructure — producing results that are misleading for Indian users and irrelevant to India's policy context.

EcoScore addresses this through a four-step assessment — transport, electricity, diet, aviation — using 2023 IPCC emission factors applied to India-specific grid data. It benchmarks results against the Indian average (1.9 tons/year) and the Paris Agreement target, identifies the user's highest-impact behaviours, and generates specific quantified recommendations rather than generic advice. A badge system, including "Paris Achiever" for users below the 2-ton threshold, makes India's NDC target personally measurable for ordinary citizens.

The governance gap: South Asian ESG reporting remains fragmented and inaccessible. Commercial ratings (MSCI, Sustainalytics) are paywalled; SEBI's BRSR mandate is recent; disclosure data is scattered across company annual reports requiring specialist interpretation. Civil society, researchers, and smaller investors have no practical comparative tool.

The platform tracks 10 major Indian corporations across a weighted ESG composite — Environmental (33%), Social (33%), Governance (34%) — with four years of historical data (2022–2025), industry benchmarking, and a Linear Regression ML layer projecting next-year performance (85–95% R²). Each company is classified as "improving" or "declining" with a confidence score. Data was manually compiled from annual reports — a process that itself built direct fluency in how BRSR compliance varies across Indian sectors and where disclosure gaps are most concentrated.
